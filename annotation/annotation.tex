\subsubsection{intro}
\begin{definition}
  
\textbf{Supra-molecular} = ``above a single molecule.'' So it is not just one molecule, but a multi-molecule assembly.

\textbf{Complex} = several parts physically interacting with each other.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[induced fit theory]
\textbf{Induced fit theory} says that an enzyme's active site is \textbf{not perfectly pre-shaped and rigid} before the substrate binds.
\end{theorem}

Instead:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The substrate first makes contact with the enzyme. 
  \item Binding interactions cause the enzyme to \textbf{change shape slightly}.
  \item The enzyme then fits more tightly around the substrate.
  \item This deformed enzyme-substrate complex is the active, stabilized state.
\end{enumerate}

So compared with the older \textbf{lock-and-key model}:

\begin{table}[H]\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{LL}
\hline
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Main idea} \\
\hline
Lock-and-key & Enzyme active site already has the exact shape for the substrate \\
\hline
Induced fit & Substrate binding causes the enzyme to reshape around the substrate \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}\end{table}

\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{results}

Starting from the completely folded structure in the global minimum, the pathway is biased along the N-terminal-to-C-terminal direction. Upon unfolding of the N-terminal $\alpha$-helix, we arrive at a local minimum (GFP$\Delta\alpha$). It is important to note that now the direction of the pathway changes, because the protein will respond to the detachment of the $\alpha$-helix by turning and reorienting along the new N-terminal-to-C-terminal direction determined by the connecting line between the first structured residue at the N terminus and the last structured residue at the C terminus of the folded portion of the intermediate. The new bias induces detachment of a $\beta$-strand leading to the second local minimum (GFP$\Delta\alpha\Delta\beta$).

\textbf{Photobleached} means the GFP molecule has been permanently damaged by light so it can no longer fluoresce.

For GFP, illumination excites the chromophore. Most of the time it returns to the ground state by emitting green light. But occasionally, the excited chromophore enters a reactive pathway, often involving oxygen or electron-transfer chemistry. That can chemically alter or break the chromophore, so the molecule becomes permanently dark. This is different from \textbf{blinking}, where GFP temporarily goes dark and then comes back. Photobleaching is usually irreversible on the experiment's timescale.

In the PNAS paper, this distinction matters because the authors show two different ``dark'' GFP states:

1. \textbf{Mechanically unfolded GFP}: dark but reversible. If the protein refolds correctly, fluorescence comes back.
2. \textbf{Photobleached GFP}: dark and not reversible by unfolding/refolding. The authors explicitly report that once GFP was photobleached, unfolding and refolding did not recover fluorescence. \href{https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1704937114}{\textbullet}

So in plain language: \textbf{unfolding turns off the GFP light switch by disrupting the chromophore's environment; photobleaching breaks the bulb.}

\subsubsection*{How redox state affects GFP fluorescence}

``Redox state'' means the balance between oxidation and reduction: whether electrons are being removed or donated in the local chemical environment. GFP fluorescence can be affected by redox chemistry in a few related but distinct ways.

\subsubsection*{1. Ordinary GFP can be damaged through oxidative photochemistry}

Photobleaching often involves the excited GFP chromophore reacting through oxygen/electron-transfer pathways. A computational/mechanistic study proposed that photoexcited GFP-type chromophores can transfer an electron to molecular oxygen, forming a chromophore--superoxide radical pair; subsequent reactions can decompose the chromophore and cause irreversible photobleaching. \href{https://iopenshell.usc.edu/pubs/pdf/jpcb-119-5444.pdf}{\textbullet}

This means oxidizing conditions or reactive oxygen species can make GFP less stable under illumination. The key point is that this is not just ``GFP senses redox''; it is \textbf{redox chemistry damaging the fluorophore}.

\subsubsection*{2. GFP can also enter reversible dark or altered fluorescent states}

GFP-family proteins have complex photophysics: blinking, protonation changes, chromophore isomerization, triplet states, and redox-related dark states can all affect brightness. The PNAS paper notes that GFP photophysical properties depend on chromophore hydrogen bonding, solvation, isomerization state, and binding-pocket structure. \href{https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1704937114}{\textbullet}

Some literature connects GFP blinking and chromophore structure to redox potentials; the PNAS reference list specifically cites Stein et al., ``Linking single-molecule blinking to chromophore structure and redox potentials.'' \href{https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1704937114}{\textbullet} The important interpretation is that \textbf{redox-related changes can shift GFP between emissive and non-emissive states}, but that is not necessarily the same as permanent photobleaching.

\subsubsection*{3. Engineered redox-sensitive GFPs deliberately convert redox state into a fluorescence readout}

The clearest redox-state literature is about \textbf{roGFP}, or reduction--oxidation-sensitive GFP. These are engineered GFP variants containing cysteine pairs that can form a reversible disulfide bond. When the cysteines are reduced, they are free thiols; when oxidized, they form a disulfide. That structural change alters the chromophore environment and shifts the excitation spectrum.

Hanson et al. designed roGFPs by introducing surface-exposed cysteines into \textit{Aequorea victoria} GFP. These sensors have two excitation maxima, around \textbf{400 nm and 490 nm}, and the fluorescence excitation ratio changes with oxidation state, allowing redox potential to be monitored in live cells. \href{https://www.jbc.org/article/S0021-9258\%2819\%2964252-3/fulltext}{\textbullet}

That ratiometric design is crucial: rather than asking ``is it brighter or dimmer?'', researchers measure the ratio of fluorescence after excitation at two wavelengths. This cancels out confounds like how much protein is present, expression level, or imaging depth. A JBC paper on mammalian-cell redox imaging states that ratios from excitation at 400 and 490 nm indicate the oxidation state and redox potential while canceling out indicator amount and absolute optical sensitivity. \href{https://www.jbc.org/article/S0021-9258\%2820\%2966844-2/fulltext}{\textbullet}

\subsubsection*{4. roGFPs are usually fused to redox-relay proteins for specificity}

A bare roGFP reports redox changes, but it may not tell you exactly which redox couple caused them. To make sensors more specific, researchers fuse roGFP to redox enzymes. For example, \textbf{Grx1-roGFP2} couples roGFP2 to glutaredoxin so it preferentially reports the glutathione redox potential, while \textbf{Orp1-roGFP} couples peroxide sensing to the roGFP readout. Reviews of genetically encoded redox probes describe these fusions as ways to target specific redox pathways such as glutathione redox potential or hydrogen peroxide. \href{https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3871076/}{\textbullet}

\subsubsection*{How this connects back to the PNAS paper}

The PNAS authors mention redox state near the end because their force-plus-fluorescence method could, in principle, be extended beyond ``is GFP folded and fluorescent?'' to ask chemical-state questions. They write that combined single-molecule manipulation and optical probing could be expanded to probe other degrees of freedom, including \textbf{redox state} or ATP binding. \href{https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1704937114}{\textbullet}

So the conceptual link is:

\textbf{GFP fluorescence depends on both structure and chemistry.}
The PNAS paper proves that native structural integrity is required for normal GFP fluorescence. Redox literature shows that the chromophore or engineered cysteine switches can also make GFP brightness/spectrum depend on oxidation-reduction chemistry.

\end{tabularx}\end{table}

\subsubsection*{1. the most interesting residue pair: \(39 \leftrightarrow 182\)}

they deliberately searched for two positions on opposite faces of the gfp \(\beta\)-barrel that were solvent accessible and could efficiently transmit tensile stress through the protein. they chose tyr\(39\) and tyr\(182\) and replaced them with the noncanonical amino acid para-azidophenylalanine, allowing the protein to be attached covalently through both positions. \href{https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/cc/c4cc07486f}{\textbullet}

so their geometry was approximately:

attachment --- residue \(39 \rightarrow\) gfp \(\beta\)-barrel/chromophore \(\rightarrow\) residue \(182\) --- attachment

rather than:

\(n\) terminus \(\rightarrow\) entire gfp sequence \(\rightarrow c\) terminus

when the elastomer bearing the doubly attached gfp was stretched by \(10\%\), \(20\%\), and \(30\%\), they measured approximately \(23\%\), \(42\%\), and \(60\%\) decreases in fluorescence, respectively. when they released the strain, fluorescence returned essentially to its initial level. \href{https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/cc/c4cc07486f}{\textbullet}
